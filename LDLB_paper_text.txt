LDLB: Label-Based Dynamic Load Balance
Algorithm for Data Center Network
Xiaoyan Qian, Rui Xu, Lei Han*, Xin He, Shuwen Wei, Qingsong Liu, Yan Gu, and Fu Xiao
Abstract—Existing switch-based load balance schemes in data
center networks face a fundamental dilemma: local strategies
respond quickly but cannot detect downstream congestion, while
global strategies approximate network-wide views but suffer from
information propagation delays. These issues prevent data flows
from quickly and accurately bypassing congested links, resulting
in prolonged Flow Completion Time (FCT). We propose Label-
Based Dynamic Load Balance (LDLB), a distributed algorithm
that bridges this gap by combining instantaneous local congestion
detection with globally synchronized congestion labels. LDLB
uses Random Early Detection and Explicit Congestion Notifica-
tion (RED/ECN) to identify congested egress ports and records
them in a local congestion table (LCT). Congested switches proac-
tively generate congestion labels—identifying affected destination
IPs—and forward them to upstream neighbors, which cache
the labels in a congestion label table (CLT). A compressible
information table aggregates contiguous IP addresses to reduce
storage and synchronization overhead. Upon packet arrival, each
switch consults both the LCT and CLT and applies a Weighted
Cost Multi-Path (WCMP)-like cost function to reroute traffic
away from congested links. Experiments on ns-3 with four real
data center workloads (Web Server, Cache Follower, Web Search,
Data Mining) demonstrate that LDLB reduces FCT by 57%,
61%, 65%, 67%, and 54% compared with ECMP, DRILL,
LetFlow, HULA, and CONGA, respectively.
Index Terms—Data Center Networks, Routing Algorithm,
Load Balance, Congestion Detection.
I.INTRODUCTION
With the advancement of network infrastructure, data center
link bandwidth has continuously increased to support diverse
and high-intensity workloads. Despite this ongoing band-
width growth, the evolution of applications poses increasing
challenges to network throughput, latency, and scheduling
efficiency[28], [29], [31]. Applications such as cloud com-
puting, real-time video, large-scale storage, and large lan-
guage models[1], [2], [3], [4] may generate bursty flows,
which require substantial bandwidth resources, sometimes
even reaching the NIC’s capacity. When the network forwards
such flows, if the switch’s egress link bandwidth is insufficient,
it can result in buffer accumulation and even buffer overflow.

These sudden congestion issues present a new challenge in
the design of load balance algorithms for data centers. Exist-
ing distributed load balance schemes in data centers can be
broadly categorized into local strategies and global strategies.
Local load balance [6], [7], [9], [10] is typically deployed
on network switches and is capable of making fast, real-
time routing decisions. However, due to their limited visibility
into downstream network conditions, they struggle to detect
and avoid congestion at downstream switches. In contrast,
global load balance [11], [45] performs path selection based
on global congestion information, offering a broader network
perspective. Nevertheless, the propagation of congestion infor-
mation incurs non-negligible delays, making it difficult to react
promptly to sudden congestion and thereby compromising
the timeliness and effectiveness of routing decisions. Table I
summarizes representative distributed load balance strategies
and analyzes the challenges they face in scenarios with bursty
congestion.
ECMP[6] distributes traffic randomly over equal-cost paths
using a hash algorithm, effective for low bandwidth usage.
However, it suffers hash polarization [5] during sudden con-
gestion, causing uneven traffic and performance degradation.
DRILL[9] performs load balance at the packet level with
fine granularity, but it requires frequent contention for limited
link resources and lacks the ability to perceive downstream
congestion. LetFlow[10] adjusts flow slice sizes solely based
on the switch’s sending rate (bandwidth) and lacks congestion-
aware ability, rendering it ineffective in handling congestion
caused by bursty traffic. CONGA[11] is a congestion-aware
load balance scheme that avoids congested links based on
global congestion information. However, due to delays in
the transmission of congestion information, it cannot respond
promptly to sudden congestion. HULA[45] is a congestion-
aware load balance scheme that uses probe packets to gather
global congestion information and avoid congested links.
However, probe packet propagation delays hinder its ability
to respond quickly to congestion changes.
According to the above analysis, current switch-based dis-
tributed load balance strategies struggle to effectively handle
sudden congestion in data center networks. To address this
challenge, we propose LDLB. This approach aims to resolve
the following key issues: (1) Data center networks carry
massive and dynamically changing traffic. In scenarios with
bursty traffic, link bandwidth fluctuates drastically. Thus, how
to quickly and accurately obtain the global congestion state
is critical for maintaining effective load balance; (2) Switches
have limited buffer resources, raising the need to design a
compressible congestion information table that reduces buffer

II.BACKGROUND ANDMOTIVATION
A.Background
Modern data centers adopt multi-rooted topologies such as
fat-tree and leaf-spine[17], [18], providing rich path diversity
Fig. 1. Load Balance Design Space
between any host pair. However, this redundancy introduces a
fundamental challenge: how to split traffic across equal-cost
paths so that no single link becomes a bottleneck. Various
works have tackled this problem from different angles —
centralized control, host-based steering, and in-network intel-
ligence — each trading off congestion awareness, reaction
speed, and resource overhead. We survey these works and
summarize them in a unified Design Space Map (Fig. 1).
Centralized approaches rely on a global controller to
compute routing decisions. Hedera[14] dynamically sched-
ules elephant flows via centralized multi-commodity flow
optimization, but suffers from scalability limitations due to
NP-hard computation and control-plane latency. WCMP[8]
extends ECMP with unequal path weights but shares the
same centralization limitation. MicroTE[49] adjusts routing
based on short-term traffic matrices. In general, centralized
schemes can theoretically achieve near-optimal placement but
are constrained by control-plane latency and single-point-of-
failure risks, making them poorly suited for sub-millisecond
reaction times.
Host-based approaches shift routing intelligence to end
hosts. PLB[60] shows that simple ECN and retransmission
signals suffice for end-host load decisions without switch mod-
ifications. Presto[12] splits traffic into fixed-size units via a
congestion-aware scheduler. Clove[47] adjusts per-path traffic
fractions based on measured congestion. However, end hosts
observe only their own traffic, lacking visibility into cross-

traffic congestion, which leads to suboptimal and oscillatory
decisions when many hosts react simultaneously.
In-network approaches embed load balance intelligence
within switches, enabling microsecond-scale reactions.
ECMP[6], the de facto baseline, uses a static hash over flow
five-tuples to map each flow to a fixed forwarding port.
It is simple and stateless but suffers from hash collisions.
LetFlow[10] randomly rehashes flowlets to different paths,
but can only respond after a new flowlet forms, limiting its
effectiveness under bursty traffic. DRILL[9] samples two
candidate output ports per packet and forwards to the shorter
queue, requiring no protocol modifications; however, multiple
switches may simultaneously choose the same path, creating
downstream congestion. TLB[48] similarly uses local queue
occupancy for per-packet spraying with reordering mitigation
at the receiver. These local schemes react quickly but are
limited by their narrow view, as a switch may inadvertently
push traffic toward an unobservable downstream bottleneck.
Global congestion-aware schemes exchange information
across switches to approximate a network-wide view.
CONGA[11] maintains congestion-to-leaf and congestion-
from-leaf tables at each leaf switch, with remote feedback
piggybacked on return traffic; however, it introduces signif-
icant state overhead and detection delays that limit scala-
bility. Hermes[46] improves upon CONGA by incorporating
path diversity. HULA[45] propagates link utilization through
probe packets, reducing per-destination memory, but its probe
dissemination introduces propagation latency. ConWeave[59]
targets RDMA by performing per-flow RTT monitoring at
the source ToR for congestion detection, constraining each
flow to at most two active paths, and reordering packets at
the destination ToR via queue pausing. LDLB takes a dif-
ferent approach by combining instantaneous local congestion
feedback with globally synchronized congestion labels. Each
switch maintains an LCT from its own port states and a CLT
populated through global synchronization, with a compressible
information table to reduce overhead. Switches update routing
based on both LCT and CLT and employ WCMP to ensure
balanced load distribution.
Despite this rich design space, existing in-network load
balance schemes still face a fundamental dilemma among
congestion blindness, state overhead, and reaction latency.
Static schemes such as ECMP and LetFlow are lightweight
but blind to congestion. Local schemes such as DRILL and
TLB respond quickly with minimal state, yet are blind to
downstream congestion. Global schemes such as CONGA and
HULA approximate a network-wide view but pay the cost
in per-destination state or probe latency. ConWeave achieves
fine-grained RDMA rerouting but requires per-flow reordering
queues and RTT monitoring. No existing solution simultane-
ously achieves fast reaction, low state overhead, and timely
global congestion awareness — a gap that motivates our work.
B.Motivation
1)Motivation for Designing Congestion-Aware Load Bal-
ance Strategy:To illustrate the hash polarization problem
inherent in randomized algorithms, we conduct an ECMP path-
selection simulation. The 5-tuple (source IP, destination IP,
Fig. 2. The location of local and global congestion within a network.
source port, destination port, and protocol) of 1000 flows are
hashed and mapped to four switch egress ports via a modulo
operation. The statistical results, shown in Fig. 3(a), indicate
that the flow distribution across the egress ports is uneven:
egress 2 carries the largest number of flows, while egress 4
carries the fewest. This outcome stems from the fundamental
property that random processes follow a normal distribution. In
real networks, flow sizes are typically non-uniform, and switch
bandwidth is constrained by various physical factors. Consider
an extreme scenario: if the physical bandwidth of egress 2
is severely limited and a long flow happens to be hashed to
that port, severe congestion will occur at that egress. This
phenomenon, where uneven hash mapping leads to overload
on specific egress ports, is referred to as hash polarization.
To further demonstrate that algorithms lacking congestion
awareness can degrade throughput, we design a small-scale
hash-polarization validation experiment based on the topology
in Fig. 2. In this experiment, a host under switch s0 sends
50 short flows (5KB each) and one long flow (100MB)
toward s4 at an aggregate rate of 100Gbps. To ensure that
the long flow dominates bandwidth usage, the link between
s0 and s2 is limited to 1Gbps, while all other links remain
at 100Gbps. We compare the conventional ECMP with the
congestion-aware LDLB (Local) algorithm. LDLB (Local) can
dynamically detect and temporarily disable congested paths,
then re-apply ECMP hashing over the remaining available
paths. As shown in Fig. 3(b), conventional ECMP, being
unaware of congestion, fails to efficiently utilize uncongested
links when path collisions occur, resulting in a significant
throughput drop. This result clearly underscores the necessity
of incorporating traffic deflection and congestion awareness
into load-balance mechanisms.
2)Motivation for Designing Global + Local Label Rout-
ing Mechanism:In networks, congestion caused by bursty
traffic and bandwidth constraints can be divided into local
congestion and global congestion. Local congestion occurs
on the egress link of the current network node and can
be directly detected and mitigated via local measurement
information. By contrast, global congestion takes place on
downstream links far beyond the current node instead of its
direct egress. As the congestion location is outside the local
scope, the node has to perceive such congestion with the help
of external signaling. Without such signaling assistance, data
traffic will continue to be forwarded along paths that are
locally idle but downstream congested, resulting in overall
network performance degradation.

III.LDLB DESIGN
A.Overview
LDLB is a distributed, in-network load balance algorithm
that combines local congestion detection with globally syn-
chronized congestion labels to avoid both local and down-
stream congested links. The overall framework is shown in
Fig. 4. We illustrate its workflow through the example shown
in Fig. 5.
Consider a leaf-spine topology where switchS2forwards
traffic toward a destination host underS4via two spine
switches,S0andS1. Suppose the egress linkP3onS1
becomes congested due to a bursty flow. LDLB operates in
three main stages:
Stage 1 — Local congestion synchronization.S1monitors
its egress queue lengths using RED/ECN. When the queue
onP3exceeds the congestion threshold,S1marksP3as
congested in its LCT. At this point,S1’s local forwarding
can already avoidP3for new flows.
Stage 2 — Global congestion synchronization.However,
S2is unaware of this downstream congestion and may con-
tinue forwarding traffic towardS1. To address this,S1gen-
erates acongestion labelidentifying the affected destination
IPs and the congested port, and sends it to its direct neighbor
S2. Upon receiving the label,S2caches it in its CLT. Now

B.Congestion Synchronization
In data center networks, timely detection and synchro-
nization of congestion information are critical for improving
load balance efficiency and optimizing link resource utiliza-
tion. The primary objective of the congestion synchronization
mechanism is to enable real-time detection, recording, and
dissemination of local link congestion states, thereby guiding
flow routing decisions and preventing new traffic from being
assigned to already congested paths. The notation and symbols
used in this paper are shown in Table II.
1)Congestion Assessment:Continuing with the example
in Fig. 5, suppose the egress queue onS1’s portP3begins
to grow due to a bursty flow. The first question LDLB must
answer is:hasP3become congested?This section presents
the RED/ECN-based method for evaluating link congestion
degree and computing marking thresholds and probabilities.
The ECN marking threshold K has the most significant impact
on network performance. Unlike the internet, data center
environments typically exhibit a lower number of concurrent
large flows[22], [23]—approximately only 2 flows at the
75th percentile. Given this limited statistical multiplexing, this
paper considers an ideal scenario where several synchronized,
long-lived flows share a bottleneck link with bandwidth C, and
these flows have the same round-trip time (RTT). As previous
research[24] has indicated, to maximize link capacity and
maintain low latency, the ECN marking thresholdKshould
be configured as follows:
K=C×RTT×λ(1)
whereλis a parameter determined by the congestion control
algorithm (e.g., for ECN,λ= 1[23]). In this paper,C×RTT×
λis referred to as the standard marking threshold for ECN.
Previous experience has shown[23] that calculating the
standard marking threshold in data centers is feasible. This is
because, unlike the internet, the RTT in data center networks
is relatively stable and can be well estimated through large-
scale measurements[19]. For example, in Microsoft’s data
centers, 23%, 74%, and 90% of the inter-rack RTTs are
approximately 200 microseconds, 300 microseconds, and 400
microseconds, respectively[23]. Additionally, since operators
have full control over the network stacks of end hosts, the
value ofλcan be easily obtained. Therefore, calculating such
a static standard marking threshold in production data centers
is quite straightforward.
RED Packet Marking Probability: In current switch chips,
ECN is typically used in conjunction with RED, which utilizes
average buffer occupancy as a congestion signal. RED has at
least three configurable parameters: the low buffer occupancy
thresholdK min, the high buffer occupancy thresholdK max,
and the packet marking probabilityP. Based on the analysis
of K in the DCTCP paper,K max andK min are derived as
follows:
Kmax =K+N,(2)
Kmin =K+N− 12 √︁2N(C×RTT +K),(3)
here,Nrepresents the number of concurrent long-lived flows
on a specific port/bottleneck link. Each flow has its own
transmission window, which jointly influences the overall
queue dynamics. The number of concurrent long-lived flows
on a port is critical for calculating the system’s overall be-
havior, especially when managing congestion and queue fluc-
tuations in multi-source scenarios. Existing research has pro-
posed various methods for estimatingN, including SYN/FIN
packet-counting[30] (largely dismissed due to insufficient ac-
curacy), complex online estimation algorithms (rarely adopted
because of high overhead), and the approach of querying the
OpenFlow flow table via an SDN controller. In our analysis
and modeling, we do not adopt the above real-time measure-
ment schemes. Instead, we directly employ the empirical value
obtained from the DCTCP paper’s large-scale measurement of
data-center traffic, i.e., the 75th percentile of the number of
concurrent large flows (>1 MB) on a server port is 2. This
value reflects the “low statistical multiplexing” characteristic
of data-center networks and is widely used as a reasonable
empirical parameter in related performance analyses.

Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) model. The basic for-

Avg= (1−ω q)×Avg+ω q ×Q,(4)
here, Avg is the average queue length at the previous time

andω q[21] is the weight factor, which controls the influence

bution. In the second method, the number of packets between
arriving marked packets follows a uniform distribution. This
paper adopts the second method. The initial packet marking
probabilityP b is calculated as a linear function of the average

2)V ertical Compression of Label Table:In a real data
center, a spine switch may serve hundreds of hosts. IfS1’s
P3is congested and affects 256 destination IPs, the congestion
label would need to list all 256 entries, and the CLT would
require 256 rows—this is impractical. Fortunately, hosts under
the same switch in data centers typically share a common IP
prefix with contiguous suffixes. LDLB exploits this property
for compression: the compression of label tables and con-
gestion labels is fundamentally aligned, with its core lying
in the efficient aggregation of IP address representations. In
data center networks, the IP addresses of hosts connected
to switches typically exhibit significant prefix similarity and
suffix continuity. In response to this characteristic, this paper
proposes a compression model based on interval representa-
tion: aggregatingmIPv4 addresses that share the same next
hop and have contiguous suffixes from individual enumeration
storage into a single table entry in the format of “common
prefix + minimum suffix + maximum suffix.” Assuming the
common prefix length iskbits, the original storage requiresn
independent entries, while after compression, only one entry is
needed. The reduction ratio in the number of entries is1− 1n,
approaching 100% whennis large. The storage overhead
per entry also decreases from a fixed32nbits to a constant
k+2×(32−k) = 64−k. The overall compression ratio can be
quantified asR= 32n64−k . In a typical scenario (k=24,n=256),
the compression ratio can reach 205 times, equivalent to
saving approximately 99.5% of bandwidth and storage space,
while reducing the number of label table entries fromnto 1,
significantly alleviating the entry pressure on switch TCAM
or SRAM. This model maintains the integrity of congestion
information while achieving a substantial reduction in control-
plane overhead in both the number and size of entries, making
it particularly suitable for large-scale data center environments
where IP addresses are allocated in contiguous blocks.
In Section IV , we evaluate the performance based on a
typical 12×8 Leaf-Spine network, where each Leaf switch
connects 32 hosts. In the uncompressed scenario (equivalent
to all IP addresses being non-contiguous), if all host IPs
(32 addresses in total) within a complete /24 subnet under
a port need to be announced due to congestion, each IPv4
address occupies 4 bytes, resulting in a single congestion
label size of 32 × 4 = 128 bytes. Under high-load conditions
with a total traffic of 6GB, the control-plane traffic accounts
for only 5MB, maintaining a control-to-data traffic ratio of
0.27%, demonstrating the low-overhead nature of the proposed
mechanism.
3)V ertical and Horizontal Compression of Label Table:
With the increasing scale of high-performance computing
in data centers, the use of IPv6 addresses is becoming a
trend. For IPv6 addresses, label compression requires the
use of Ternary Content Addressable Memory (TCAM). First,
zero-compression is used to initially process the IP address.
This method is particularly suitable for IPv6 addresses, as it
removes consecutive zeros from the address, achieving a mod-
erate reduction in address length. Next, prefix overlapping is
used to reduce the vertical entries in the congestion label table.
Finally, prefix minimization is introduced, which employs the
Espresso-II algorithm to achieve deeper compression. Prefix
minimization focuses on analyzing the prefix information in IP
addresses, identifying and removing redundant parts, thereby
further optimizing the size of IP addresses. This comprehen-
sive compression strategy not only improves storage efficiency
but also facilitates subsequent network processing and data
transmission.

To improve hard-failure detection, the design can integrate

from the candidate next-hop set.
Congestion-delay Timer:To mitigate transient inconsis-
tency between local and global congestion states, we intro-
duce a congestion-delay timer at core switches. During the
interval between local detection and global synchronization,
upstream switches may still forward traffic toward newly
congested core switches, potentially causing transient drops
due to unavailable next hops. Therefore, a congested link is
not disabled immediately. Instead, a delay timer is started,
with duration determined by the ratio of congestion-label
size to link bandwidth. During this window, the link remains
temporarily available, allowing time for global label propaga-
tion and synchronization, thereby reducing synchronization-
induced packet loss.
2)Local Label Routing:Consider a local congestion sce-
nario: one ofS2’s own egress portsP x becomes congested
(marked as “C” in the LCT).S2simply queries the LCT before
applying ECMP hashing, temporarily removingP x from the
candidate port set so that new flows are automatically directed
to other uncongested ports. ECMP, a widely used local load-
balance method, distributes flows by applying a hash-modulo
operation to the flow five-tuple:
i=h(flow.fivetuple) modk,(10)
whereh(flow.fivetuple)denotes the hash function,iis the
selected path index, andkis the number of candidate paths.
This strategy can suffer from hash collisions and uneven
hash-value distribution. As a result, multiple flows may be
mapped to the same path, overloading some links while
leaving others underutilized. The problem is particularly pro-
nounced under local congestion, where ECMP may continue
forwarding traffic onto already congested paths.
Our mechanism mitigates this issue by consulting the
switch-maintained LCT before final path selection. Ports
marked as congested are temporarily removed from the routing
table and are restored once congestion is cleared.
To further improve stability, we employ flow pinning to
avoid routing oscillation and intra-flow packet reordering
caused by rapid state transitions (congested/non-congested).
Each switch maintains a per-port active-flow table that records
the five-tuples of currently forwarded flows. When a port
becomes congested, existing flows on that port are pinned
to it, ensuring in-order delivery. Only newly arriving flows
are allowed to react to updated congestion states and select
alternative uncongested paths. This design preserves packet
ordering while retaining load-balance flexibility for new traffic.
We quantify the effect of flow pinning on the 12×8 leaf-
spine topology under a mixed workload of Cache Follower
and 50:1 Incast traffic, with offered loads of 0.3 and 0.9. As
shown in Fig. 6, at load 0.3, LDLB (packet), which does not
use these stability mechanisms, exhibits about 15% worse tail
FCT than LDLB (flow). At load 0.9, LDLB (packet) achieves
better average metrics but incurs approximately 1% flow loss
(47 out of 7,753 flows). In some scenarios, LDLB (packet)
without stability safeguards even performs worse than ECMP
in both latency and loss. These results highlight the necessity
of the proposed mechanisms for stable label-routing operation
under dynamic, high-variance traffic conditions.

mechanism—namely, the inability of upstream switches to

congestion at the current switch and fails to address global
congestion scenarios where upstream switches may continue
to forward traffic to already congested downstream switches.
To address this gap, we propose a global label-based routing
mechanism. This mechanism actively propagates congestion
labels generated by congested downstream switches to all
their upstream switches. Upon receiving these global labels,
upstream switches can update their routing tables by integrat-
ing both local congestion information and the received global
labels, thereby enabling proactive avoidance of downstream
congested links.

Traffic Workload:To evaluate LDLB under different con-
gestion conditions, we designed two traffic scenarios.
The firstis a persistent-congestion scenario. We inject
long-lived background flows and reduce the bandwidth of
selected links to emulate (i) local congestion (leaf egress
congestion), (ii) global congestion (spine egress congestion),
and (iii) hybrid local-global congestion. This asymmetric
configuration, where only selected links are throttled while all
others remain at full capacity, intentionally creates controlled
and reproducible congestion points that emulate the scenario
where long-lived flows persistently occupy specific links —
precisely the worst case that LDLB is designed to address.
The bandwidth is gradually reduced from 60 Gbps to 20
Gbps to systematically evaluate LDLB’s congestion detection
and rerouting effectiveness under varying congestion intensi-
ties while isolating the impact of individual congested links.
Hosts simultaneously generate short flows as target traffic for
performance evaluation. Under the hybrid local-global setting,
we further generate target traffic using four representative real-
world workloads: Web Server (WSv), Cache Follower (CF),
Web Search (WSc), and Data Mining (DM).The secondis a
bursty-congestion scenario, where we use a traffic pattern of
“four real-world workloads + 500 KB burst aggregation” to
emulate transient congestion events. Unless otherwise spec-
ified, traffic load is controlled by adjusting the flow arrival
rate; flow arrivals follow a Poisson process. Source-destination
host pairs are selected at random. All compared algorithms are
evaluated under identical traffic conditions to ensure fairness.
Evaluation Metrics:FCT and its cumulative distribution
function (CDF) are core metrics in our experimental evalua-
tion. FCT measures the end-to-end latency of an individual
flow from initiation to completion, while the CDF charac-
terizes the overall distribution of FCT, capturing its central
tendency and dispersion, and revealing tail-latency behavior.
LDLB is compared with six representative load balance
algorithms (ECMP, DRILL, LetFlow, CONGA, HULA and
ConWeave) across distinct congestion scenarios.
B.Performance Under Persistent Congestion Scenarios
1)Local Congestion Scenario:To evaluate the perfor-
mance of LDLB (Local) under persistent local congestion,
the experiment first injects multiple long flows as background
traffic to continuously occupy selected links. Short flows are
then generated within the terminal cluster associated with the
long flows as foreground traffic for evaluation. Meanwhile, the
bandwidth of the leaf-switch egress links traversed by long
flows is gradually reduced from 60 Gbps to 20 Gbps, while
all other links remain fixed at 400 Gbps. This setup creates
sustained congestion with different intensities on multiple leaf
egress links, thereby emulating a local-congestion environment
in data center networks. In addition, the experiment employs
RoCEv2, which is based on UDP, as the transport protocol.
To ensure reliable transmission can still be maintained under
congested conditions, sufficient buffers are configured at the
senders, receivers, and switches to minimize the impact of
packet loss and retransmissions caused by congestion on
transmission performance.
As shown in Fig. 7(a)–(d), we compare LDLB (Local)
and ECMP across three congestion levels. The congested link
bandwidth is 20 Gbps in (a), 40 Gbps in (b), and 60 Gbps in
(c). At 20 Gbps (Fig. 7a), ECMP’s tail extends substantially
while LDLB maintains a compact distribution. The root cause
is that ECMP’s static hash continues assigning traffic to
the bottleneck port with the same probability as to healthy
ports, causing compounding queue buildup. LDLB breaks this
cycle by detecting the congested queue via RED/ECN and
removing the port from the candidate set. At 40 Gbps (Fig. 7b),
ECMP’s CDF shifts rightward with a visible tail, while LDLB
(Local) reduces average FCT by 38% by excluding congested
ports. A distinctive knee appears in LDLB’s CDF near the
tail—this corresponds to flows that, after the congested port
is removed, are hashed onto remaining ports that still ex-
hibit residual imbalance from ECMP’s hash distribution. At
60 Gbps (Fig. 7c), the CDF curves nearly overlap, confirming
that LDLB’s congestion-detection overhead is negligible under
light load.
2)Global Congestion Scenario:Since LDLB (Local) lacks
global congestion awareness, we further develop a global
label-based routing mechanism and systematically evaluate
the effectiveness of LDLB (Global) under global congestion
scenarios. Specifically, multiple long flows are first injected
as background traffic to continuously occupy selected links,
after which short flows are introduced as foreground traffic.
Meanwhile, the bandwidth of spine-switch egress links tra-
versed by long flows is gradually reduced from 60 Gbps to 20
Gbps, while all remaining links are fixed at 400 Gbps. This
setup creates different levels of congestion on spine egress
links, thereby emulating a global congestion environment in
data center networks.

3)Local + Global Congestion Scenario:In real-world
data centers, network topology and traffic dynamics are highly
complex and time-varying. To better approximate practical
deployments, we construct a composite scenario that in-
cludes both local and global congestion, and introduce het-
erogeneous link capacities to create non-equivalent multi-
hop paths. Specifically, multiple long flows are first injected
as background traffic to continuously occupy selected links,
after which short flows are introduced as foreground traffic.
Meanwhile, the bandwidth of spine and leaf egress links
traversed by long flows is gradually reduced from 60 Gbps
to 20 Gbps, while other links remain unchanged. This setup
induces different congestion intensities on multiple spine and
leaf egress links, thereby forming a joint local-and-global
congestion environment.
As shown in Fig. 9(a)–(d), the result trends are consistent
across all subfigures: a critical finding is that LDLB (Local)
performs over 10% worse than ECMP at 20 Gbps. The
root cause is that purely local awareness cannot perceive
downstream spine congestion—it sees no queue buildup on
its own leaf egress ports and continues forwarding traffic
into the bottlenecked spine link. ECMP, though congestion-
unaware, occasionally outperforms LDLB (Local) purely by
hash randomness. LDLB (Global+Local) resolves this fun-
damental limitation by combining local RED/ECN detection
for leaf congestion with globally propagated labels for spine
congestion, improving FCT by over 50% versus ECMP and
over 2×other baselines with the most compact CDF across
all subfigures.
The consistent ranking across bandwidth levels reveals how
each scheme’s mechanism determines its performance under
combined congestion: CONGA and HULA, despite possessing
global awareness, suffer from feedback delays that limit their
responsiveness; DRILL and LetFlow, being local-only, are
simultaneously blind to both leaf and spine congestion. At
60 Gbps, several schemes show FCT degradation relative to
ECMP because their continuous control overhead (queue sam-
pling, flowlet tracking, probes) exceeds congestion-avoidance
benefits under light load, while LDLB generates zero control
traffic on uncongested ports.
Overall, these results demonstrate that LDLB provides sig-
nificant advantages in complex scenarios with non-equivalent
links and simultaneous local-global congestion. It more ef-
fectively utilizes residual bandwidth, thereby reducing overall
FCT while improving network throughput.

As shown in Fig. 10(a)-(d), a clear pattern emerges across
all four workloads. At 60 Gbps and 40 Gbps, DRILL, LetFlow,
HULA, and CONGA all exhibit higher average FCT than
ECMP—their control overhead yields insufficient congestion-
avoidance benefit under light congestion. As the bottleneck
tightens to 20 Gbps, these schemes transition from degradation
to improvement over ECMP, as congestion avoidance finally
outweighs overhead. LDLB achieves the lowest FCT at all
bandwidth levels without any degradation regime.
Notably, HULA and CONGA perform consistently worse
than DRILL and LetFlow despite having global awareness.
The root cause is twofold. First, HULA and CONGA incur
continuous control traffic (probes, per-flowlet feedback) that
consumes bandwidth on congested links, while DRILL and
LetFlow add negligible overhead. Second, in this persistent
fixed-bottleneck scenario, global feedback staleness means
congestion information often arrives too late to affect in-
flight traffic, whereas DRILL’s local sampling and LetFlow’s
flowlet randomization react immediately at the switch. The
overhead of global coordination thus yields no proportional
benefit under sustained fixed-point congestion.
LDLB avoids both pitfalls. Its label mechanism is trig-
gered only upon congestion detection, generating zero control
traffic on uncongested ports—eliminating the degradation at
60/40 Gbps. Its proactive one-hop label notification delivers
congestion signals in sub-RTT without waiting for reverse
traffic or probe cycles. Combined with simultaneous local
and global awareness, LDLB covers both leaf and spine
bottlenecks, unlike local-only or delayed-global schemes.
This paper conducts evaluations using the UDP-based Ro-
CEv2 transport protocol. Although RoCEv2 provides reliable
transmission guarantees at the link layer, its performance is
highly dependent on lossless network conditions. Under the
congestion scenarios constructed in the experiments, even
with lossless mechanisms such as PFC configured, high-
intensity background traffic may still cause transient exhaus-
tion of switch buffers or trigger PFC backpressure, leading
to micro-burst packet loss or latency. This effect is ampli-
fied in throughput-sensitive flows such as Web Search and
Data Mining, resulting in worse average FCT compared to
Cache Follower, which is more latency-sensitive and exhibits
different traffic characteristics. If TCP were used in the same
network topology, its end-to-end congestion control and packet
loss retransmission mechanisms, while ensuring data integrity,
may further increase the average FCT for these throughput-
sensitive workloads due to higher latency and protocol over-
head. Therefore, in RDMA networks, the co-design of the
transport protocol, underlying lossless network mechanisms,
and load-balance algorithms is crucial for guaranteeing both
high throughput and data integrity.
C.Performance Under Burst Congestion Scenarios
1)Test of Congestion Label Compression:We implement
a lightweight congestion-label compression mechanism (CLC)
in ns-3 by aggregating labels with the same IP prefix into a
single entry and retaining only the minimum and maximum
values for contiguous suffix ranges. This design reduces label
overhead while preserving essential congestion semantics. To
evaluate both the effectiveness of CLC and the coupling
behavior between LDLB and DCTCP, we build a data center
network based on Topology 2 and conduct comparative exper-
iments on four representative workloads. We compare LDLB,
LDLB/CLC, LDLB+DCTCP, and LDLB/CLC+DCTCP under
two load levels (0.3 and 0.9). Flow arrivals follow a Poisson
process, host pairs are randomly matched, and an additional
500 KB, 50:1 incast pattern is superimposed to emulate bursty
many-to-one congestion.
As shown in Fig. 11 and Fig. 12, ECMP performs worst
overall across workloads and load levels, indicating that
static hash-based forwarding is insufficient for incast-induced
transient hotspots. Compared with ECMP, LDLB is more
robust under different configurations (with/without DCTCP
and with/without CLC), achieving stable gains in both Avg
FCT and P99 FCT, with peak improvements up to 90%.
After enabling CLC, LDLB shows no degradation relative to
the uncompressed baseline and further achieves an additional
0.2%–44% FCT improvement (under the assumption that
hosts under the same leaf share the same prefix and contigu-
ous suffixes). Although CLC introduces modest computation
overhead, it substantially reduces control-plane label traffic,
improves label lookup and congestion-table update efficiency,
and lowers switch memory usage.

2)Real Workload With Incast:To systematically char-
acterize performance differences across workload types and
load levels, we conduct comparative experiments on Topology
2 against CONGA, DRILL, LetFlow, and ConWeave under
four representative data-center workloads. Two load settings
(0.3 and 0.9) are considered. Traffic arrivals follow a Poisson
process, host pairs are randomly matched, and an additional
500 KB, 50:1 incast pattern is superimposed to emulate bursty
many-to-one congestion.
As shown in Fig. 15 to Fig. 16, LDLB achieves about 30%–
60% performance gains over CONGA, DRILL, and LetFlow
in most scenarios. Compared with the newer congestion-aware
scheme ConWeave, LDLB is overall comparable and further
outperforms it in several workload/load combinations. It is
worth noting that the ranking of algorithms is not identical
across all workloads; however, LDLB and ConWeave consis-
tently remain in the first tier. These trends can be explained by
differences among the five schemes in congestion-awareness
capability, decision granularity, and rerouting execution mech-
anisms.
CONGA performs path-aware load balance with flowlet-
level rerouting at the source ToR. Although it improves over
static hashing, its effectiveness depends on flowlet boundaries;
in long-flow-dominant scenarios, insufficient flowlet gaps limit
rerouting opportunities, and reverse-path-dependent feedback
is sensitive to network dynamics, resulting in slower conver-
gence and less stable tail latency under persistent hotspots.
DRILL adopts a lightweight queue-aware local decision strat-
egy with low overhead, but limited global congestion visibility
can lead to local-optimum/global-suboptimum behavior under
burst contention. LetFlow relies on natural flowlet gaps and
randomized spreading; while scalable, it provides limited
proactive suppression of synchronized hotspots in high-load
incast settings, and thus often underperforms in both Avg FCT
and P99 FCT. ConWeave combines fine-grained congestion
feedback with fast rerouting and in-network reordering, typ-
ically delivering faster convergence and stronger tail-latency
control; however, in long-flow-heavy workloads (e.g., Data
Mining), where congestion is persistent rather than transient,
the marginal gain of aggressive dynamic rerouting decreases
and control/coordination overhead becomes more pronounced.
LDLB performs path avoidance using explicit congestion la-
bels and can be coupled with CLC and DCTCP: CLC reduces
control-plane overhead and accelerates notification dissem-
ination/table updates, while DCTCP provides end-host rate
regulation under sustained congestion. Consequently, LDLB
remains in the same first tier as ConWeave and is often
superior when stable congestion avoidance and low control-
plane overhead are both required.
In summary, algorithm ranking is jointly determined by
three capabilities: fast and accurate congestion awareness, ro-
bust congestion decision and path avoidance, and controllable
rerouting side effects. This also explains workload-dependent
ranking shifts: under short-flow burst-dominant conditions,
ConWeave and LDLB are usually strongest; under long-flow
persistent-congestion conditions, LDLB often shows better
robustness.

in Cache Follower and Web Search, where label redundancy
is relatively high, CLC effectively reduces control overhead
through signal compression, and its benefits outweigh the
processing delay cost. In Data Mining, persistent large flows
lead to network bandwidth saturation, and the slight processing
delay introduced by CLC prevents it from achieving a net
gain in such bandwidth-constrained environments. In Web

and decompression delays introduced by CLC, though small,

congestion states. As a result, some short flows may be
misdirected to congested links before optimal path information
takes effect, thereby undermining tail latency optimization.

in Fat-Tree, the extent of its advantage and the additional gains

timeliness and overhead. Future work may explore workload-
adaptive strategies to further enhance the generality of the

V. RELATEDWORK
In recent years, a variety of congestion control mechanisms
have been proposed to address diverse network scenarios.
QCN[36] quantifies congestion at the link layer for Ethernet,
while DCQCN[37] combines ECN marking with PFC sup-
pression for RDMA. TIMELY[38] and Swift[39] use RTT as a
congestion signal for delay-aware rate control. MP-RDMA[40]
proposes a multi-path acknowledgment clock scheme for
RDMA. HPCC[33] employs INT-based telemetry for per-hop
congestion detection and precise rate adjustment. PCN[41]
regulates only identified congested flows rather than all traffic,
and TCD[42] detects congestion in ongoing transmissions.
BFC[43] applies per-flow, hop-by-hop backpressure to elimi-
nate Head-of-Line blocking, while Dart[44] decomposes con-
gestion relief into receiver-side and in-network sub-tasks.
Since LDLB relies on RED/ECN for local congestion detec-
tion, the accuracy of ECN marking directly affects its routing
decisions. In multi-queue switch architectures, per-port ECN
marking may cause non-congested queues to be erroneously
marked due to buffer pressure from other queues sharing the
same output port. ECN+[54] proposes a marking-aware opti-
mization that excludes already-marked packets from the output
port buffer’s marking decision, thereby preventing fault mark-
ing. DC-ECN[52] leverages Gaussian Process Regression to
separate mice and elephant flows into dual couple buffers, each
with independently tuned dynamic ECN thresholds (K min
andK max). Priority-ECN[55] combines the marking-aware
strategy with priority scheduling for marked packets and a Cut
Payload mechanism, ensuring that congestion signals reach
the sender early for timely rate adjustment. These techniques
can complement LDLB by improving its local congestion
detection accuracy in multi-queue environments.
LDLB can further benefit from DRILL’s[32] “power-of-
two-choices” principle for route updates and HPCC’s[33]
telemetry-based congestion assessment. It can also syn-
ergize with centralized schemes[12], [13] or centralized
controllers[14], [15], and incorporate dynamically tunable
ECN thresholds[50], [51], [53].
VI. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we proposed LDLB for data center networks.
LDLB bridges the gap between local and global load balance
by combining instantaneous local congestion detection (via
RED/ECN and the LCT) with globally synchronized conges-
tion labels (stored in the CLT). A compressible information
table reduces label storage and synchronization overhead,
and a WCMP-like cost function enables congestion-aware
